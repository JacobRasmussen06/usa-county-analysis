Introduction

In the United States, there are 3,109 counties/county-equivalents across the continental part of the country (the ‘lower 48’ and the District of Columbia). These counties stretch from the mega city environment of New York County, New York, to the most sparse rural life of Loving, Texas. Every county has something unique to offer, but there are patterns you see in some counties. Pick a random county, and it is likely pretty similar to counties nearby, which raises an interesting question: how are counties similar and different from each other.

More specifically, can we group counties together meaningfully with publicly available data? This project aims to answer that question.

Traditional geographic classifications often rely on simpler statistics, like population or their status as urban or rural, it’s evident there are more complex patterns beneath the surface. Unsupervised machine learning provides an opportunity to uncover these patterns and use them to group counties together.

This project develops a pipeline of data that contains information on a county’s demographics, economics, education, employment, crime, politics, geography, and climate, explores the makeup of the United States based on the variables in the pipeline, and then explores three different approaches to cluster the counties: principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering (HC or HCA), and Gaussian mixture model clustering (GMM). Through these approaches, meaningfully significant archetypes of U.S. counties were found, and an engine of similar and unique counties was created.

Finally, the results of this project were synthesized to create a dashboard and an interactive map so that readers can more interactively explore the counties of the United States.

Data Documentation

Several different data sources were processed and cleaned to craft a final dataset. To see the detailed dictionary of all the variables in the dataset, refer to Appendix A.

Data Sources

American Community Survey (ACS)

The American Community Survey 2023 5 year estimates served as the primary source of the demographic, housing, economic, and educational characteristics of counties. Variables like median household income, median age, poverty rate, educational attainment, and much more were obtained from the ACS. Furthermore, the 2018 ACS was also used to construct 5 year changes in income and population, the latter of which being used to construct a population stability index. A diversity index was also engineered using race data from the ACS.

TIGER/Line Geography

TIGER/Line county boundaries were used as the backbone for all geographic visualizations throughout the project. In addition to county boundaries, TIGER/Line was the data source for a county’s land area and its water coverage percentage.

PRISM Climate Normals

PRISM Climate normals were processed and used to characterize long-term conditions in a county’s climate. Mean annual temperature and annual precipitation were obtained from this data source.

USGS Elevation

Via the R package elevatr, USGS elevation rasters were obtained and summarize to produce a county’s mean elevation and the ruggedness (standard deviation) of its terrain.

NLCD / FIA Land Cover

National Land Cover Database and Forest Inventory and Analysis products were used to estimate forest coverage, the amount of land in a county that is forested.

County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (CHR&R)

The CHR&R was the data source for the homicide rate, suicide rate, and firearm fatalities of a county, as well as its voter turnout in the 2020 election.

MIT Election Data and Science Lab County Presidential Election Returns

The MIT Election Data served as the data source for the political variables of the dataset. These variables include the percentage of votes the Democratic party earned in the 2020 election, as well as three engineered variables: party competitiveness, and 20 year changes in both democratic vote share and party competitiveness.

Rural Urban Continuum Codes

The USDA Rural Urban Continuum Codes were used to effectively distinguish metropolitan counties from rural counties, validate the results of a particular decision made, and provide standardized metrics of urbanization among counties.

Overall, the data pipeline for this project was thorough, containing several data sources and steps. First data was obtained in raw form. Then, it was cleaned, and variables were engineered. Then, every source was merged into one dataset using a county’s 5 digit identifier, referred to as its GEOID in the project. Following the merger, the dataset was used as the backbone to clustering analysis. Finally, the dataset was used as an engine to be used in the accompanying Shiny Dashboard and Interactive Map. The full pipeline of data can be found in the first three scripts of the scripts folder, found in the accompanying GitHub repository.
